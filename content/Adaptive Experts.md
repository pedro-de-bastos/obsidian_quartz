Schwartz et al make a distinction between teaching to improve efficiency versus preparation for innovation. This distinction underlies the differentiation between strong in [[Preparation For Learning (PFL)]] versus being efficient in solving [[Sequestered Problem Solving]] type of problems. 

### How 'Efficiency' Helps with 'Innovation'

According to the authors, while an over-emphasis on efficiency can sometimes hinder flexibility, efficiency is a crucial component of innovation for several reasons:

- **Frees Up Cognitive Resources:** Efficiency allows individuals to process routine tasks automatically, which frees up "attentional bandwidth". This enables learners to concentrate their mental energy on the novel aspects of a problem that require adaptation and innovation.
    
- **Prevents Overwhelm:** In complex environments, having efficient, automatized routines prevents people from becoming overwhelmed by basic tasks, allowing them to keep sight of larger goals.
    
- **Provides a Necessary Foundation:** Innovation "favors the prepared mind," meaning that having a well-organized, fluently accessible set of skills (efficiency) is often a prerequisite for successful adaptation. While efficiency alone is insufficient for innovation, it remains an important ingredient.
    

### Preparation for 'Adaptive Experts'

The authors define **adaptive experts** as those who are highly efficient at routine tasks but can also adapt to novel situations, learn new information, and "let go" of old efficient methods when necessary.

To prepare students to become adaptive experts, the authors suggest the following strategies:

- **Balancing Efficiency and Innovation (The OAC):** The authors propose an "Optimal Adaptability Corridor" (OAC), arguing that instruction should balance and interleave activities that promote both innovation and efficiency rather than focusing on them in isolation.
    
- **Prioritizing Early Innovation:** The authors present evidence that "early innovation yields better adaptability in the short run and better efficiency in the long run".
    
    - For example, in a study comparing students learning fractions with tiles (which required innovation to interpret) versus pies (which were intuitively efficient), the "tile students" who had to innovate first showed a more stable learning trajectory and eventually surpassed the "pie students" in efficiency.
        
- **Encouraging Interaction:** Preparation involves opportunities to actively interact with artifacts and other people. These interactions help reveal new information and force learners to "let go" of efficient but incorrect initial interpretations.
    
- **"Working Smart" Assessments:** The authors suggest using "working smart" goals where students must innovate tools (like graphs or spreadsheets) to solve repetitive problems efficiently. This combines the need for innovation (creating the tool) with the goal of efficiency (solving the problem quickly).
